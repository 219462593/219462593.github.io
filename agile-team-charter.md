## Reflection

Co-creation. We built the charter iteratively, not in one workshop. First, each member wrote expectations and non-negotiables on a shared whiteboard — silent brainstorming so louder voices didn't dominate. We clustered themes (communication, accountability, quality, availability) and each person drafted one section for group review. A real disagreement arose over how to handle missed deadlines: one member wanted a strict "three strikes" escalation; another found it punitive. We reframed the question from "how do we punish failure?" to "how do we catch problems early?". That produced our 48-hour early-warning agreement, so that if a flag is anticipated ahead work can be redistributed. This was a synthesis, not a compromise.

Why these agreements. Core hours (12:00–14:00 weekdays) were the only overlap across all five schedules; we limited them to synchronous work so asynchronous tasks stayed flexible. Our Definition of Done (acceptance criteria met) was chosen because two members have a skill gap in backend development, peer review doubles as knowledge transfer. We chose consent-based decisions (no blocking objection) over consensus after consensus-seeking cost us 40 minutes on a naming convention.

Living document. In Week 20, a member missed two stand-ups and a deliverable without flagging it, violating the 48-hour rule. Instead of enforcing punishment, we held a retrospective conversation. The root cause was assessment overload and embarrassment. We amended the charter with a "red flag" protocol (mark yourself red on Trello, no explanation needed, tasks redistributed) and added a mandatory "what's blocking me" field to stand-ups. We now review the charter for 15 minutes at each sprint end.

Impact. Before our Sprint 2 demo, a key feature wasn't working. Because our decision-making protocol was pre-agreed, we called a 15-minute meeting and decided within 10 minutes to drop the feature and document it as a known limitation. Without the charter, that discussion would likely have taken an hour and ended in tension. The charter's real value was giving us pre-agreed rules for how to disagree.

What i learned is that a charter only works when it's treated as a living artefact — the moment we stopped defending the original document and started improving it was when it actually reduced friction.
